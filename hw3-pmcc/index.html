Poor Man's Covered Call: a backtest

Apple and MP Materials, weekly calls against a Jan-2028 LEAP, 2025-11-03 to 2026-10-30. Data: LSEG Workspace (expired option quotes, hourly). FINTECH 535 · Felipe Jaramillo.

1. The answer

  • Less capital, as advertised, but leveraged. The PMCC tied up Apple 36%, MP Materials 48% of the covered call's capital. A ~0.80-delta LEAP moves like 80 shares on 30–50% of the money, so returns and drawdowns on that capital are larger in both directions.
  • Apple (AAPL) (buy-and-hold 29.2%): covered call $7,399 on $29,033 (25.5%); PMCC $5,508 on $10,445 (52.7%) with the covering rule, $7,637 (90.1%) with the textbook rule. Max drawdown: covered call 9%, PMCC 21% / 34%.
  • MP Materials (MP) (buy-and-hold −31.4%): covered call $172 on $5,770 (3.0%); PMCC $228 on $2,756 (8.3%) with the covering rule, −$1,929 (−70.0%) with the textbook rule. Max drawdown: covered call 32%, PMCC 42% / 79%.
  • The strike rule decides what the strategy is. The textbook rule (short strike ≥ LEAP strike + LEAP cost) almost never allows a weekly call against a two-year LEAP, so that PMCC is mostly a leveraged long call: it won in AAPL's rally and lost 70.0% in MP's fall. With only the covering rule it is an income strategy: in MP the weekly premium more than paid for the LEAP's loss at mid fills (but not after paying half the spread); in AAPL's rally it gave up the upside every week the stock ran through the strike.
  • Verdict: a PMCC is not a cheaper covered call; it is a covered call on margin. Use it when you want covered-call exposure on less cash and can stomach drawdowns of 20–80% of that cash. On a wide-spread name like MP the edge depends on getting filled near the mid (section 5).

2. The strategy, from first principles

2.1 Covered call

Own 100 shares at S0, sell one weekly call with strike Ks for premium p. At expiry the buyer takes any gain above Ks:

$$\text{Capital}_{CC} = 100\,S_0, \qquad \text{P\&L} = 100\,\big[\min(S_T, K_s) - S_0 + p\big]$$

AAPL, 3 Nov 2025: 100 × 267.52 = $26,752.

2.2 PMCC: replace the shares with a LEAP

A LEAP is a call that expires a year or more out. Buy one deep in the money (strike KL well below S) and sell the same weekly call against it. Its price is two pieces:

$$\text{Capital}_{PMCC} = 100\,C_L, \qquad C_L = \underbrace{(S-K_L)}_{\text{intrinsic}} + \underbrace{\text{time value}}_{\text{the rent}}$$

AAPL, 3 Nov 2025, S = 267.27: the Jan-2028 220 call cost 80.85 = 47.27 intrinsic + 33.58 time value, so $8,085 instead of $26,752 (30% of the shares). About 17.86 of that time value is interest: you pay KL in 2028, not today, worth KL(1 − e−rT) = 220 × (1 − e−0.0382 × 2.22).

2.3 Delta: how many shares the LEAP acts like

$$\Delta = e^{-qT}N(d_1), \qquad d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r - q + \tfrac12\sigma^2)\,T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}$$

N is the standard normal CDF: the area under the bell curve to the left of d1. d1 is roughly a z-score: how many units of σ√T the stock sits above the strike. q is the dividend yield, r the 3-month T-bill rate. Delta is the option's price change per $1 in the stock, so 100Δ is its share-equivalent.

AAPL LEAP: σ = 29.9% (implied from its mid), T = 2.22 years, Δ = 0.80, so it moves like 80 shares. With a 0.30-delta weekly sold against it, the position is 100(ΔL − Δs) ≈ 80 − 30 = 50 shares.

Delta changes with S, but how fast depends on σ√T, the denominator of d1. For this LEAP σ√T = 0.44, so a 5% stock move shifts d1 by about 0.05/0.44 ≈ 0.11 and delta barely moves. A one-week call at the same volatility has σ√T ≈ 0.035: the same 5% move swings it from near 0 to near 1.

2.4 Leverage

$$\frac{dC_L}{C_L} \approx \lambda\,\frac{dS}{S}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{\Delta\,S}{C_L}$$

AAPL: λ = 0.80 × 267.27 / 80.85 = 2.64. In the data, AAPL fell 7.7% from 3 Nov 2025 to 2026-03-30; the LEAP fell 22.0%, from 80.85 to 63.02. In dollars the LEAP lost less than 100 shares would have (Δ < 1); as a percent of its capital it lost 2.9× as much (more than λ, because implied volatility also fell).

2.5 The strike rule

Worst case at a weekly expiry: you are assigned at Ks and exercise the LEAP to deliver the shares. Per share:

StepCash
Bought the LEAP−D
Sold the weekly call+p
Assigned: sell at Ks+Ks
Exercise the LEAP: buy at KL−KL
$$\text{P\&L} = K_s - K_L - D + p \;\ge\; 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; K_s \ge K_L + D - p \;\;\xrightarrow{\;p\,\to\,0\;}\;\; K_s \ge K_L + D$$

The textbook version drops p, because premiums from earlier weeks may already have gone to buybacks.

AAPL: KL + D = 220 + 80.85 = 300.85, which is 12.6% above the stock on day one. No one-week call that far out pays anything, so this PMCC sold its first call only after AAPL rallied (section 5). The rule makes the strategy a leveraged long call most of the time.

Two refinements. The cost D is sunk, so a forward-looking rule uses today's LEAP price CL. And you would sell the LEAP rather than exercise it; deep in the money it is worth S − KLe−rT. After a large rally the short call is worth S − Ks, so the week cannot lose money if

$$K_s \;\ge\; K_L e^{-rT} + C_L - p \qquad \text{(jump-safe rule, tested in section 6)}$$

The minimal rule is the course's covering rule: a long call covers a short call only if KL ≤ Ks and the LEAP expires later. Without it the position is a call spread that needs margin.

2.6 Friday: buy back, don't get assigned

Near Friday's close an in-the-money weekly is worth about its intrinsic value:

$$C_s \approx S - K_s \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{buyback cost} \approx \text{what assignment costs}$$

Same expected cost, but assignment adds risk for nothing: you are short 100 shares over the weekend (a Monday gap hits you), that short needs Reg T margin, and AAPL call holders may exercise early ahead of an ex-dividend date. So the algorithm buys back.

AAPL covered call, week of 2026-02-02: sold the 267.50 call for 1.25. Friday's 14:00 bar showed AAPL at 278.56; it bought the call back for 10.78 ≈ 278.56 − 267.50 = 11.06. The shares rose by the same amount, so the week gave up the gain above the strike but did not lose money.

2.7 Accounting

$$\text{NAV} = \text{cash} + 100\,C_L - 100\,C_s + n_{\text{shares}}\,S$$

A short option's mark is subtracted. Reg T: long stock needs 50% initial and 25% maintenance margin; the LEAP is paid in full; a covered short call adds nothing; short stock after an assignment needs 50% and 30%. Capital is the most cash a strategy ever tied up (the long leg, plus any buybacks not yet paid for by premiums). Return on capital = P&L / capital.

No look-ahead. LSEG hourly bars are stamped at their start; a bar's bid/ask are the last quotes before it ends. Every decision uses one bar and every fill uses a later bar. The LEAP and the weekly call are chosen from that Monday's quotes; the floor rules use yesterday's LEAP close; deltas use the trailing dividend and the prior day's T-bill rate. Expiries come from the real trading calendar (Good Friday, Juneteenth and July 3 weeks expire Thursday). The two stocks were picked by hand, which is a hindsight choice; nothing else is.

Fill check. Across every option bar that also printed a trade, the last trade regressed on the mid has slope ≈ 1, intercept ≈ 0 and R² > 0.99 (table above), so the mid is a fair fill on average. Spreads are what the sensitivity tests.

Validation. validate.py re-derives the books: cash reconciles to the blotter row by row, NAV equals its parts, Reg T arithmetic, every option fill sits inside the quoted bid/ask of a later bar on a contract whose RIC encodes that expiry and strike, outcomes agree with the tape (assigned only if the close is above the strike, bought back only if the stock was above it), the LEAP always covers the short call, and every position is flat at the end. All checks pass on both stocks.

git clone https://github.com/felipejpenagos/fintech535 && cd fintech535/hw3-pmcc/src

python3 probe.py AAPL.O

LSEG keeps about one year of hourly history, so a re-fetch later will cover a later window; the cached raw_*.json files in the repo are what this page was built from.

8. Limits

  • One year and two stocks: one rising path and one falling path, not a distribution.
  • Hourly bars: the fill is the quote at the end of the next bar, not a guaranteed execution at that price.
  • Early exercise is modeled only at expiry; a holder could exercise an in-the-money AAPL call the day before an ex-dividend date. The Friday buy-back limits this but does not rule it out.
  • Delta comes from a European model on American options; it is used only to rank strikes, never to price fills.
  • Margin is Reg T as taught in the course; a broker's house rules or portfolio margin would differ.
  • One LEAP held all year, no rolls; a LEAP that falls far out of the money (MP) is not replaced.
